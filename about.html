About Liscere

What Liscere is

Liscere is an industrial cybersecurity company. We help industrial and critical infrastructure operators protect the systems that run their physical processes, and we work on three fronts at once:

  • Security services: OT and ICS security assessments, OT visibility and monitoring, NIS2 readiness and incident response for OT.
  • Automation engineering: PLC and HMI projects built secure from the start, review and modernisation of existing automation, and industrial training.
  • Research and development: new tools for industrial security, starting with a framework that judges whether an industrial command fits the state of the process.

Each front feeds the others. What we see in the field shapes the tools we build and the automation we deliver, and the research behind those tools sharpens every assessment.

About us

Liscere was founded in Poland in 2026, born from a clear view that industrial cybersecurity has become urgent. We work globally, wherever critical systems need protecting.

Why industrial security matters

Water treatment, energy, manufacturing and transport all run on control systems that were built to keep a process running, not to defend themselves. Many of them cannot be patched, restarted or replaced without stopping the process they control. When they are attacked, the consequences are physical: a line stops, a dose changes, equipment is damaged or people are put at risk.

The attacks have changed too. Adversaries increasingly reach these networks through legitimate access and then act like operators, issuing commands that pass every technical check. Our threat landscape briefing sets out this shift from access to action.

Regulation is catching up, and NIS2 now makes cybersecurity a management duty for essential and important entities across the EU. The systems that matter most are often the least protected. That is why we work on them.

The framework we are building

Alongside our services, we are developing a framework for contextual evaluation of industrial control actions. Its guiding idea is a short one: authorised is not always appropriate. A command can pass every identity, access and protocol check and still be wrong for the current state of the physical process.

The framework observes a copy of the OT traffic from a switch mirror port and never sits in the control path. From that traffic it reconstructs the actions taken, infers the operational phase of the process and judges whether each action fits that phase. Its goal is to be vendor and protocol agnostic, and it adds to the controls already in place: it does not replace firewalls, network segmentation or access control.

The framework is in active development and validated in our laboratory. We publish what we learn in our research.
