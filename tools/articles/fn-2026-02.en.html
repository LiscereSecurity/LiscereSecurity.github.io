FIELD NOTES · FN-2026-02 · 17 November 2026

Patched, unpatched, unsupported: the refrigeration attacks came back in November

Three weeks after the French supermarket campaign, attackers reached refrigeration controllers again, this time at food businesses in the United Kingdom and Belgium. The vulnerabilities they used were not new. A CISA advisory had named them in February, and the manufacturer had published a fix. The equipment was still reachable from the internet, still running the old firmware, and in one product line it will never run anything else.

What happened

  • The second wave. In the early hours of Monday 2 November 2026, refrigeration controllers were attacked at three British meat businesses, all members of the Association of Independent Meat Suppliers, and at Langridge Organic, a fruit and vegetable wholesaler. Belgium's cyber security agency was handling cases at the same time.
  • The equipment. The trade body said its members were attacked through vulnerable Copeland XWEB supervisory controllers. A security note it circulated named firmware version 1.12.1 and earlier, and described authentication bypass flaws that let an attacker take control without a password.
  • The damage is disputed. Reports said stock had spoiled, but the trade body said none of its affected members lost meat, and the wholesaler said its own team intervened before anything was lost. Both statements can be true: these attacks are survivable if somebody is watching at three in the morning.
  • No attribution. Nobody claimed the November wave, and British authorities have not attributed it. A Belgian spokesperson said it may never be possible to identify who was behind it. This is a contrast with October, when France named a group it links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard.
  • The official read. The UK National Cyber Security Centre said the national impact was minimal, and said its team worked through the night to contact businesses whose systems were exposed.
  • The manufacturer. Copeland said certain XWEB monitoring solutions are affected by the reported vulnerability, and that it had re-communicated its recommended patching and security protocols.

The fix was not new

On 26 February 2026, eight months before the November attacks, CISA published advisory ICSA-26-057-10 covering Copeland XWEB and XWEB Pro controllers at version 1.12.1 and earlier. It listed 23 vulnerability identifiers. The most severe, CVE-2026-21718, carries a CVSS v3.1 base score of 10.0 for pre-authenticated code execution, which is the highest score the scale produces. CISA recorded that the manufacturer had provided a fix.

The security note circulated to British meat suppliers in November described an update as available since August. The dates given in public differ, and we have not reconciled them. The point survives either way: a correction for these flaws existed for months before the controllers were attacked, and the controllers were attacked anyway.

The line that will not be fixed

The security firm VulnCheck published research on 13 November 2026 describing 14 further vulnerabilities, CVE-2026-124002 through CVE-2026-124015, in a different product line, the Copeland XWEB EVO. By its account one of them is an unauthenticated request that executes a parameter as root, and another chains a command injection that runs before the password is checked with a setuid interpreter to reach root. It also describes a backdoor administrator password and daily credentials derived from the device's MAC address and the date.

VulnCheck states that a Copeland bulletin from March 2026 declared the XWEB EVO family end of software support, with no fixes after 16 March 2026, and counts between one and two thousand EVO systems reachable from the internet against roughly a hundred of the supported Pro line. We have not confirmed those counts or that date against a second independent source, and we say so rather than repeat them as settled.

Our analysis

Five technical observations, from reading the advisories rather than the coverage.

Count the code paths that run before authentication, not the CVEs. The February advisory lists 23 identifiers, which sounds alarming and mostly is not: the majority score 8.0 and require a valid session, which means they matter only after something else has already failed. The ones that decide the outcome are the handful that need nothing: CVE-2026-21718, scored 10.0 for pre-authenticated code execution, CVE-2026-24663 at 9.0 for unauthenticated remote execution through a library installation path, and CVE-2026-25085 at 8.6 for authentication bypass. In the EVO research the same shape appears more starkly. VulnCheck describes a command injection in the username field of login.lua that executes before the password is checked, and a CGI endpoint, xwconsole.cgi, that runs a request parameter as root with no session at all. When you assess a device of this class, the number worth writing down is how many entry points execute attacker-controlled input ahead of the credential check. On these controllers that number is not zero, and one is enough.

Credentials derived from a MAC address and a date are not credentials. VulnCheck reports three identifiers covering daily passwords computed from the device's MAC address and the current date, alongside a hardcoded administrator password. This is a recurring embedded pattern, usually introduced so a service engineer can get in when a customer has lost the password, and it has a property that ordinary weak credentials do not: there is nothing to rotate. A MAC address is not secret. It is broadcast in every frame on the local segment, it is frequently visible in the device's own web interface, and it is often derivable from the serial number. A date is public by definition. Recover the derivation once and you hold a working credential for every device in the family, on every future day, permanently. Any hardening guidance that begins "change the default password" does not apply here, because the vulnerable secret is an algorithm rather than a password.

A setuid interpreter is the tell. The EVO chain that VulnCheck describes reaches www-data through the login injection and then escalates to root through a Lua interpreter carrying the setuid bit. That second step is more informative than the first. A setuid interpreter is not an accident of configuration; it is what remains when a developer separated privileges, found that the web layer still needed to perform a privileged action, and reopened the door rather than narrowing the action. For the defender it has a practical consequence: any compensating control that assumes the web process is unprivileged is void on this device. There is no useful distinction between "the web interface was compromised" and "the controller was compromised", so network position has to carry the whole burden that privilege separation is not carrying.

A CVSS 10.0 on a process supervisor is not the same event as a 10.0 on a server. CVSS scores the confidentiality, integrity and availability of the vulnerable component. It has no axis for the physical process behind that component, so a maximum score on a refrigeration supervisor and a maximum score on a public web server arrive at the same number while meaning different things. Worse, the inverse also holds: an attacker who obtains a valid session needs no vulnerability at all to cause the damage, because writing a setpoint is the device's intended function and would score nothing. This is the gap IEC 62443-3-2 is built to close, by assessing consequence against the process and its tolerable risk rather than against the asset. A severity figure from a vulnerability database is an input to that assessment, not a substitute for it.

The supervisor is a dual-homed conduit endpoint and should be the most hardened device on site, not the least. A supervisory controller of this class exists to bridge two worlds: an HTTP interface for people and a field bus, commonly Modbus or a vendor protocol, down to the case controllers. That is its purpose and also its vulnerability class. Compromise of the HTTP side yields field write capability with no further exploitation, because issuing setpoints is what the device is for. In the terms of IEC 62443-3-3 it sits on a conduit between zones of very different trust, and FR 1 and FR 5, identification and authentication control and restricted data flow, are the requirements that land on it hardest. In practice that means it belongs in its own zone, reached only through a VPN terminated outside it, with writes to field devices observed for rate and range. That design is also the answer for a product line that will receive no further fixes. When patching is no longer available, the remaining controls are segmentation, a separate credential path, monitoring of what is sent, and a recorded replacement date. Those are compensating controls in the 62443 sense, and they should be written down and assessed as such rather than left as intentions.
