FIELD NOTES · FN-2026-02 · 17 November 2026

Patched, unpatched, unsupported: the refrigeration attacks came back in November

Three weeks after the French supermarket campaign, attackers reached refrigeration controllers again, this time at food businesses in the United Kingdom and Belgium. The vulnerabilities they used were not new. A CISA advisory had named them in February, and the manufacturer had published a fix. The equipment was still reachable from the internet, still running the old firmware, and in one product line it will never run anything else.

What happened

  • The second wave. In the early hours of Monday 2 November 2026, refrigeration controllers were attacked at three British meat businesses, all members of the Association of Independent Meat Suppliers, and at Langridge Organic, a fruit and vegetable wholesaler. Belgium's cyber security agency was handling cases at the same time.
  • The equipment. The trade body said its members were attacked through vulnerable Copeland XWEB supervisory controllers. A security note it circulated named firmware version 1.12.1 and earlier, and described authentication bypass flaws that let an attacker take control without a password.
  • The damage is disputed. Reports said stock had spoiled, but the trade body said none of its affected members lost meat, and the wholesaler said its own team intervened before anything was lost. Both statements can be true: these attacks are survivable if somebody is watching at three in the morning.
  • No attribution. Nobody claimed the November wave, and British authorities have not attributed it. A Belgian spokesperson said it may never be possible to identify who was behind it. This is a contrast with October, when France named a group it links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard.
  • The official read. The UK National Cyber Security Centre said the national impact was minimal, and said its team worked through the night to contact businesses whose systems were exposed.
  • The manufacturer. Copeland said certain XWEB monitoring solutions are affected by the reported vulnerability, and that it had re-communicated its recommended patching and security protocols.

The fix was not new

On 26 February 2026, eight months before the November attacks, CISA published advisory ICSA-26-057-10 covering Copeland XWEB and XWEB Pro controllers at version 1.12.1 and earlier. It listed 23 vulnerability identifiers. The most severe, CVE-2026-21718, carries a CVSS v3.1 base score of 10.0 for pre-authenticated code execution, which is the highest score the scale produces. CISA recorded that the manufacturer had provided a fix.

The security note circulated to British meat suppliers in November described an update as available since August. The dates given in public differ, and we have not reconciled them. The point survives either way: a correction for these flaws existed for months before the controllers were attacked, and the controllers were attacked anyway.

The line that will not be fixed

The security firm VulnCheck published research on 13 November 2026 describing 14 further vulnerabilities, CVE-2026-124002 through CVE-2026-124015, in a different product line, the Copeland XWEB EVO. By its account one of them is an unauthenticated request that executes a parameter as root, and another chains a command injection that runs before the password is checked with a setuid interpreter to reach root. It also describes a backdoor administrator password and daily credentials derived from the device's MAC address and the date.

VulnCheck states that a Copeland bulletin from March 2026 declared the XWEB EVO family end of software support, with no fixes after 16 March 2026, and counts between one and two thousand EVO systems reachable from the internet against roughly a hundred of the supported Pro line. We have not confirmed those counts or that date against a second independent source, and we say so rather than repeat them as settled.

Our analysis

Five things in this case are worth saying plainly, because we have not seen them said elsewhere.

Refrigeration is an unusually good target, and the reason is physics rather than software. Thermal mass makes the attack slow and quiet. An attacker does not need to hold access, only to change a setpoint and leave; the cold store does the rest over several hours while nobody is on site. Alarm thresholds on this equipment are generally tuned to detect a failing compressor, not an operator command that is formally valid, so the temperature can drift a long way inside the band the alarm considers normal. Defrost makes it worse, because a long defrost is the one condition where rising temperature is expected and alarms are routinely suppressed. And the damage lands in inventory, not in the plant: afterwards there is no broken equipment to examine, only warm food and a controller that will tell you it did exactly what it was asked.

"Patch faster" is the wrong lesson, and repeating it will not help anyone. Updating a supervisory controller is not updating a server. It frequently needs a site visit, a window when the process can tolerate a restart, a contractor with the right credentials and, in regulated food environments, a change record. Honest patch latency on this class of equipment is measured in quarters, not days. A fix published in February reaching a cold store by November is not an outlier; it is close to the normal rate. If that is true, then an estate strategy built on patching promptly is a strategy built on something that does not happen. The control that would have prevented both waves is the cheap one and the boring one: the controller should not have been reachable from the internet at all. CERT-FR said this first in October, and it remains the only recommendation that works regardless of firmware version, vendor or support status.

End of support means something different in a food plant than in an office. A supervisory controller is not replaced because it has a CVE. It is replaced when the refrigeration plant around it is replaced, and that is a capital cycle of fifteen years or more. So telling an operator to migrate off an unsupported line is, in practice, telling them to do nothing for a decade. The useful engineering answer is to accept that the device stays and to design around it: put it behind a boundary it cannot be reached through, give it a separate credential path, watch what is sent to it, and write down the date on which it goes rather than leaving replacement as an aspiration. That is less satisfying than a migration plan, and it is what actually happens.

The second wave may not be the same adversary, and that is the uncomfortable part. October was attributed to a state-linked group. November was claimed by nobody, and VulnCheck characterises the scanning it observed as opportunistic. The pattern we would expect, and which this is consistent with, is an aftershock: once a capable actor demonstrates publicly that a sector's controllers are reachable and that a specific product family is the way in, the method becomes common knowledge and the follow-on does not need the original skill or the original motive. For an operator this inverts the usual comfort. A firm that concluded in October that it was too small to interest a state is precisely the firm the aftershock finds.

The obligation exists whether or not anyone has listed the device. Food production, processing and distribution are sectors covered by NIS2, and the duty to manage risk does not come with an exemption for equipment the IT department has never seen. Yet a refrigeration controller is rarely on an IT asset register at all. It arrived with the plant, it is maintained under the refrigeration service contract, and the remote connection exists because the contractor needs to see alarms without driving to site. IT does not know the device exists; the contractor follows compressor manufacturers rather than security advisories. The notice and the person who could act on it never meet. The same gap runs through building management, weighing and batching, packaging lines and effluent control, and it is why an inventory that stops at the IT boundary gives a false sense of completeness. Under an audit, equipment missing from the inventory is harder to defend than a known vulnerability that was assessed and accepted.

A fix you have not applied is not a fix.
