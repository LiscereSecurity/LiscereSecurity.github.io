Working with Liscere

What does Liscere do?

Liscere is a European industrial cybersecurity company, headquartered in Poland and working globally. We provide security services and secure automation engineering to industrial and critical infrastructure operators, and we develop new tools for industrial security, starting with a framework for contextual evaluation of industrial control actions.

Which services does Liscere offer?

Two groups of services. Industrial and OT security: OT and ICS security assessments, OT visibility and monitoring, NIS2 readiness and incident response for OT. Automation engineering: automation projects (PLC and HMI programming, hardware and commissioning), review and modernisation of existing automation, and industrial training. Each one has its own page, linked from the services page on the services page.

Why does a cybersecurity company also build automation?

Because many OT security problems are built in from day one: open access to controllers, flat networks, no backups. Designing automation with security from the start avoids them, and our security work shows us where projects usually go wrong.

Who does Liscere work with?

Industrial and critical infrastructure organisations of any size: manufacturing plants, utilities, energy, water and wastewater, transport and building systems, and the suppliers that serve them.

Where does Liscere work?

Globally. Liscere is a European company headquartered in Poland, and takes on work in Europe and beyond. Our NIS2 readiness work covers the EU, where each member state applies the directive through its own national law.

Can Liscere help us prepare for NIS2?

Yes. Our NIS2 readiness assessment maps each obligation to what you have today, gathers OT visibility as evidence, prepares an incident playbook for OT and organises an audit readiness pack. Questions of legal interpretation stay with your counsel, and we are glad to work alongside them. See NIS2 readiness.

Will an assessment disrupt our production?

No. On live OT networks we observe rather than probe: nothing is scanned, injected or changed while the process is running. Active testing, such as penetration testing, runs only on systems and in time windows agreed with your team.

How does an engagement start, and how is it priced?

With a short scoping call: you tell us what you run and what worries you, and we tell you honestly whether we are the right fit and what a sensible first step looks like. Scope and price are then fixed up front, and you receive a prioritised report in plain language with the evidence attached.

The framework and research

What is contextual evaluation of industrial control actions?

It is the idea behind the framework Liscere is developing: judging an action not only by whether it is permitted, but by whether it fits the operational context of the process at that moment. An action can be authorised, protocol conformant and within valid ranges and still be inappropriate for the current operational phase. The framework infers that phase from the observed traffic and judges whether the action is appropriate for it.

Is the framework available as a product?

Not yet. The framework is in active development and validated in our laboratory. Organisations that want to see it on their own network can apply for a contextual anomaly detection pilot; slots are limited.

How is the framework different from an intrusion detection system?

An intrusion detection system inspects the traffic; the framework inspects the process. A signature based IDS matches packets against patterns of known attacks, and an anomaly based IDS flags protocol violations or statistical deviations from a network baseline. Both look for traffic that is malformed, unauthorised or unusual.

The framework instead reconstructs the operational state of the plant from the observed process variables and evaluates each action against that state. The events it surfaces are well formed, permitted and statistically ordinary, which is precisely why traffic level sensors have no reason to flag them. It is a semantic layer over the process, not another detection rule over the packets.

Does the framework sit in the control path?

No. It is an external observer. It connects to a switch mirror port, receives a copy of the traffic and injects nothing; it cannot send, block or alter any command, and it is electrically incapable of reaching the control path.

Does the framework depend on a specific vendor or protocol?

No. Being vendor and protocol agnostic is a design objective. The framework evaluates the process rather than any one manufacturer's implementation, so it is not bound to a particular controller, fieldbus or SCADA stack. Because it only observes a copy of the traffic, nothing is installed on the controllers, nothing is reconfigured, and no new path is opened into the control network.

How is the framework validated?

On real industrial hardware, under realistic conditions, and across a growing range of processes and protocols. The work runs on controllers used in industry, the Siemens S7 family, with real engineering configurations, over protocols including Modbus/TCP and OPC UA, and every result is exercised across many operational variants rather than a single case.
