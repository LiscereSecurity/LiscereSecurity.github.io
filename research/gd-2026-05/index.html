The directive did not set your deadline: how national transposition fixes the real clock

NIS2 told member states what to require. It did not tell your plant when. The dates that bind an operator come from the national act, and they vary by country, by entity type and by obligation. Poland's transposition is now in force and fully dated, which makes it a useful worked example of what to look for in your own: when the obligations actually bite, when the first audit lands, when a penalty first becomes possible, and which requirements mean something different on a production line than in an office.

Key points

  • The directive set no date for an operator. Three dates come from the national act and are rarely the same: when obligations become enforceable, when the first audit is due, and when penalties become available.
  • In Poland those are 3 April 2027 for the chapter 3 obligations, 3 April 2028 for the first audit of essential entities, and 3 April 2028 before any financial penalty can be imposed.
  • The scope sentence decides whether OT is included. Poland ties the management system to the information system used in the processes affecting provision of the service, so if stopping the line stops the service, the line is in scope.
  • Three requirements read differently on a plant than in an office: updates demand a documented impact and criticality assessment rather than prompt patching, cutting inbound traffic is explicitly permitted, and the hardest supply chain link is the maintenance contractor with remote access.

What the Polish timetable looks like

The amending act was published on 2 March 2026 and, under its article 49, entered into force one month later, on 3 April 2026.

Article 33(1) gives entities that met the criteria on that date twelve months to perform the obligations of chapter 3, so 3 April 2027. Article 33(2) gives essential entities twenty four months for the first audit, so 3 April 2028. Registration in the national list closed on 3 October 2026, a date set not by the act but by a ministerial schedule the act requires.

Article 35 is the one most commentary leaves out: the financial penalties cannot be imposed for the first time until two years after entry into force, which is again April 2028. There is a real deadline four and a half months away and a separate, later date at which it becomes expensive. Confusing the two produces either panic or complacency, and neither buys any security.

The lesson generalises. Three dates matter in any member state and they are rarely the same: when the obligations become enforceable, when the first audit is due, and when sanctions become available. Ask for all three.

The scope sentence is the one to read twice

Poland's article 8(1) requires an information security management system "in the information system used in the processes affecting the provision of the service by that entity".

That phrasing settles an argument that is still open in a lot of plants. Scope follows the service, not the department. If stopping the line stops the service, the line is in scope, and so are the PLCs, operator stations, historian, field network and engineering workstation that run it, whether or not anybody has put them on an asset register.

Equivalent wording appears across transpositions because it comes from the directive's own framing. It is worth finding the exact sentence in your national text, because it is the sentence that decides whether an auditor accepts "that equipment belongs to the maintenance contractor" as an answer.

Three requirements that do not mean what an IT reader assumes

Updates are not a mandate to patch everything. The Polish text requires regular software updates "in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, taking into account an analysis of the update's impact on the security of the service provided and the criticality level of individual updates".

Read closely, that is not an instruction to patch promptly. It is an instruction to be able to show that criticality and impact were assessed, and that deferring an update was a decision rather than an omission. This matters because prompt patching of a supervisory controller is frequently not achievable: it needs a site visit, a window where the process tolerates a restart, a contractor with credentials and, in regulated environments, a change record. Honest patch latency on this class of equipment runs to quarters. A documented impact assessment plus a compensating control is defensible in front of an auditor. An empty update register is not, and neither is a policy promising a patch window nobody has ever used.

Cutting network traffic is explicitly permitted. The same article requires immediate action on discovering a vulnerability, "including the temporary limitation of inbound network traffic to the infrastructure", while taking into account the effect of that limitation on the availability of services.

Disconnection is named in the statute as a legitimate response rather than tolerated as a last resort. The practical consequence is that you need to know, in advance and by testing, what happens to the process when remote access is cut: which alarms stop arriving, which contractor can no longer see the plant, what runs unattended. A decision taken at three in the morning without that knowledge is usually a decision to do nothing.

Supply chain means the maintenance contractor, not the software vendor. The requirement covers the security and continuity of ICT products, services and processes on which service provision depends, "taking into account the relationship between the direct supplier of hardware or software and the entity", and the following paragraph requires considering vulnerabilities associated with that supplier and the general quality of their products and processes.

In a plant the hardest link is rarely the office software vendor. It is the firm with remote access to the refrigeration plant, the boiler house or the packing line. That equipment arrived with the installation, is maintained under a service contract and is unknown to the IT department. The obligation reaches that relationship whether or not the service contract mentions it.

Where to start, with a deadline in months

Inventory first, because every other requirement depends on it and none of them can be evidenced without it. Include equipment that sits under service contracts rather than under IT. Then establish what of yours answers from the internet, as opposed to what should. Then assess risk from the process rather than the device: the question is what happens to the service, not whether a controller has a known flaw. Only then choose controls, because only then does proportionality have anything to rest on.

Document as you go. An audit under these regimes does not test whether you are secure. It tests whether you can show why you did what you did.

Questions

Does NIS2 itself give my company a deadline?

No. The directive required member states to transpose it, and the dates that bind an operator come from the national act. They differ by country, by entity type and by obligation, so the useful question is not when NIS2 applies but what three dates your national law sets: when the obligations become enforceable, when the first audit is due, and when sanctions first become available.

What are the Polish dates?

The amending act was published on 2 March 2026 and entered into force on 3 April 2026 under its article 49. Article 33(1) gives entities twelve months for the chapter 3 obligations, so 3 April 2027. Article 33(2) gives essential entities twenty four months for the first audit, so 3 April 2028. Article 35 provides that financial penalties cannot be imposed for the first time until two years after entry into force, again April 2028. Registration in the national list closed on 3 October 2026, a date set by a ministerial schedule rather than by the act.

Are industrial control systems in scope?

Where the scope sentence ties the obligation to the service, yes. The Polish act requires the management system in the information system used in the processes affecting the provision of the service, which means scope follows the service rather than the department. If stopping the production line stops the service, the line is in scope, along with the controllers, operator stations, historian, field network and engineering workstation that run it.

Does the law require us to patch promptly?

The Polish text requires updates in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, taking into account an analysis of the update's impact on the security of the service and the criticality of individual updates. That is a requirement to assess and record, not to patch immediately, which matters because prompt patching of a supervisory controller is often not achievable. A documented impact assessment plus a compensating control is defensible; an empty update register is not.

Who counts as the supply chain for a plant?

Not primarily the office software vendor. The obligation covers ICT products, services and processes on which the service depends, taking into account the relationship between the direct supplier of hardware or software and the entity.
