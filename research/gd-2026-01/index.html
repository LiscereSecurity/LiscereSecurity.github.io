NIS2 and Poland's KSC act: who is covered and what the deadlines are

NIS2 applies in Poland through the amended national cybersecurity system act, which entered into force on 3 April 2026. The question most companies ask is not what NIS2 is, but whether it applies to them. This guide answers that directly: who is in scope, what has to be in place, by when, and what it means for control systems.

Key points

  • NIS2 covers medium and large entities in the sectors of Annexes I and II, and suppliers are increasingly asked for evidence too.
  • In Poland the amended KSC act has been in force since 3 April 2026; risk management measures are due by 3 April 2027.
  • The deadline to apply for entry in the register of essential and important entities passed on 3 October 2026.
  • The obligations cover information systems, and in a plant that includes controllers, operator stations and the control network.

Who NIS2 covers

Scope depends on two things at once: the sector and the size of the company.

  • Sector. Annex I of the directive lists sectors of high criticality: energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, waste water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, public administration and space. Annex II lists other critical sectors: postal and courier services, waste management, chemicals, food, manufacturing (medical devices, electronics, machinery, vehicles and other transport equipment), digital providers and research.
  • Size. As a rule, medium and large entities are covered, meaning at least 50 staff or an annual turnover or balance sheet above 10 million euros. For some services, such as domain name registries and trust service providers, the size threshold does not apply.

Entities are classified as essential or important. The difference affects supervision: essential entities face proactive supervision, important entities mainly reactive supervision, after an event.

A company outside those lists can still be affected indirectly. Supply chain security is one of the obligations, so covered customers increasingly ask their suppliers for evidence, whatever their size.

How it works in Poland

Poland transposed the directive through the act of 23 January 2026 amending the national cybersecurity system act, published on 2 March 2026 and in force since 3 April 2026. Two dates are often confused and are worth separating.

  • 3 April 2026: the act entered into force. The new system started on that day, including the register of essential and important entities.
  • 3 October 2026: the deadline passed for entities that met the criteria when the act took effect to apply for entry in the register. A company that has not applied should do so now rather than wait to be asked.
  • 3 April 2027: the deadline for putting risk management measures, procedures and organisational structures in place.
  • April 2028: according to published guidance, administrative fines can in most cases only be imposed two years after the act entered into force.

The thresholds that make an incident "significant" are to be set in a regulation for each sector. As of September 2026 the draft covered fifteen sectors. Until the new regulation is issued, the existing thresholds apply.

What has to be in place

The directive sets out a minimum set of risk management measures. In practice that means documents, processes and evidence that they are used:

  • risk analysis and information system security policies,
  • incident handling,
  • business continuity, including backups and disaster recovery,
  • supply chain security, including relationships with suppliers and integrators,
  • security in acquiring, developing and maintaining systems, including vulnerability handling,
  • assessment of how effective the measures are,
  • basic cyber hygiene and training,
  • cryptography and encryption,
  • human resources security, access control and asset management,
  • multi-factor authentication and secured communications.

That list is the same across the Union. National differences concern supervision, deadlines and penalties, not the obligations themselves.

Reporting incidents

A significant incident is reported in three steps: an early warning within 24 hours of detection, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report within one month of the notification. Trust service providers have 24 hours for the notification itself. Reports go to the relevant sector CSIRT and, until it declares operational capability, to CSIRT NASK, CSIRT GOV or CSIRT MON.

The clock runs through weekends and nights. The details, including what to do when the incident affects a production line rather than office systems, are covered in a separate guide on reporting OT incidents.

Management accountability

NIS2 places duties on management bodies: approving the risk management measures, overseeing their implementation and taking part in training. Members of management can be held liable for failures. That is a change from the previous regime, where cybersecurity was often treated as a matter for the IT department alone.

What it means for OT

The law speaks of "information systems", and in a plant those include the PLCs, operator stations, SCADA systems and the networks between them. That is the part compliance projects tend to leave out.

Three practical consequences:

  • Risk analysis without an OT inventory is incomplete. It is hard to assess risk for devices that appear on no list. As built documentation rarely matches reality after years of upgrades.
  • An IT incident procedure does not work in OT. Disconnecting an infected workstation is simple. Disconnecting an operator station in the middle of a batch is not, and the decision belongs to process engineering rather than IT.
  • Evidence has to cover the control network. A supervisor asks for specific records: what is on the network, who has remote access to it, how changes to controller configuration are detected.

Where to start

  1. Establish whether the company is covered. Check the sector against Annexes I and II and the size threshold. Where it is unclear, confirm it with a lawyer, because the classification has formal consequences.
  2. Check the entry in the register. If the company meets the criteria and no application was filed, the deadline has passed and it needs to be made good.
  3. Build an inventory, including OT. A list of devices, connections and remote access is the base of every step that follows.
  4. Write an incident reporting procedure with real roles, phone numbers and a decision rule that works within 24 hours.
  5. Plan the work towards 3 April 2027 and collect evidence as it goes, not the week before an audit.

Questions

Who does NIS2 cover?

Medium and large entities, meaning at least 50 staff or more than 10 million euros of turnover or balance sheet, in the sectors listed in Annexes I and II of the directive: energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, water, waste water, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, public administration, space, postal services, waste management, chemicals, food, manufacturing, digital providers and research. Some services are covered regardless of size.

When did Poland's KSC act start to apply?

The act of 23 January 2026 amending the national cybersecurity system act was published on 2 March 2026 and entered into force on 3 April 2026. Entities that existed at that point have until 3 April 2027 to put risk management measures in place.

Who has to register?

Entities that meet the criteria apply for entry in the register of essential and important entities. For those covered when the act entered into force, the deadline was 3 October 2026, so a company that has not applied should do so without waiting to be asked.

What are the fines?

The directive sets a maximum of 10 million euros or 2% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, for essential entities, and 7 million euros or 1.4% for important entities. Published guidance on the Polish act indicates that administrative fines can in most cases only be imposed from April 2028.

Does NIS2 cover OT systems?

Yes. The obligations apply to information systems, and in an industrial company those include PLCs, operator stations, SCADA and the networks between them. Compliance work that stops at the office network leaves the part that runs the physical process uncovered.
