FIELD NOTES · FN-2026-01 · 12 November 2026

Authorised commands, spoiled food: the October 2026 supermarket refrigeration attack

On the night of 11 to 12 October 2026, attackers reached the refrigeration controllers of around 700 supermarkets in France. They did not break the fridges. They used the controllers' own functions, defrost cycles and temperature setpoints, to warm fridges and freezers overnight until the food was lost. The equipment did exactly what it was told.

Key points

  • Around 700 supermarkets in France were hit on the night of 11 to 12 October 2026, and controllers from a second manufacturer the following night.
  • Nothing was broken. The attackers changed defrost cycles and temperature setpoints, normal functions the controllers are built to accept.
  • Food losses are estimated at tens of millions of euros. On 9 November France attributed the operation to a group it links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard; Tehran rejected the accusation.
  • The lesson for any OT operator: remove internet exposure first, then ask whether each command makes sense for the process at that moment.

What happened

  • Scale. French authorities put the number of affected supermarkets at about 700, out of roughly 10,000 in the country. Early press reports spoke of 250 to 350 stores before the official figure was given.
  • What was changed. The attackers altered defrost and temperature settings. One retailer said its refrigeration units ran at 50 °C for about six hours. Le Monde reported defrost cycles lengthened from one hour to as many as eight, which delayed alarms. At one store near Bordeaux, a freezer setpoint was changed from −22 °C to 150 °C, with several settings altered per second, which points to automation.
  • Consequences. Food losses are estimated at tens of millions of euros. The Paris prosecutor's office said the attack also caused fires.
  • A second wave. On the night of 12 to 13 October the campaign reached controllers from a second manufacturer. CERT-FR updated its alert to cover them.
  • Access. How the attackers got in has not been publicly confirmed. Researchers counted thousands of refrigeration controller interfaces reachable from the internet, many of them on end of life systems.
  • Attribution. On 9 November France publicly attributed the operation to a group it links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Tehran rejected the accusation.

Why this matters beyond supermarkets

Nothing in this attack required the fridges to malfunction. Defrost is a normal function. Changing a setpoint is a normal action for a technician. Every command arrived in the format the controller expects, through the interface it was built to accept. Seen one by one, the commands were authorised.

What made them harmful was context: a defrost stretched to eight hours in the middle of the night, a freezer asked to reach +50 °C, the same change pushed to many cabinets within seconds. The question that would have caught them is not "is this command allowed?" but "does this command make sense for this process, right now?"

The same pattern applies to water treatment, energy, hospitals and factories. Wherever a controller can be reached, an authorised command can be used against the process it controls.

What CERT-FR recommends

  • Take the affected controllers off the internet.
  • Restore remote access only through a secure VPN or a protected network.
  • Use IP filtering only as a temporary emergency measure.
  • For compromised end of life devices, do not rely on a factory reset alone.
  • Review defrost schedules.

Removing exposure comes first. Monitoring does not replace it.
