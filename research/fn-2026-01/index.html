FIELD NOTES · FN-2026-01 · 12 November 2026

Authorised commands, spoiled food: the October 2026 supermarket refrigeration attack

On the night of 11 to 12 October 2026, attackers reached the refrigeration controllers of around 700 supermarkets in France. They did not break the fridges. They used the controllers' own functions, defrost cycles and temperature setpoints, to warm fridges and freezers overnight until the food was lost. The equipment did exactly what it was told.

Key points

  • Around 700 supermarkets in France were hit on the night of 11 to 12 October 2026, and controllers from a second manufacturer the following night.
  • Nothing was broken. The attackers changed defrost cycles and temperature setpoints, normal functions the controllers are built to accept.
  • Food losses are estimated at tens of millions of euros. On 9 November France attributed the operation to a group it links to Iran’s Revolutionary Guard; Tehran rejected the accusation.
  • The lesson for any OT operator: remove internet exposure first, then ask whether each command makes sense for the process at that moment.

What happened

  • Scale. French authorities put the number of affected supermarkets at about 700, out of roughly 10,000 in the country. Early press reports spoke of 250 to 350 stores before the official figure was given.
  • What was changed. The attackers altered defrost and temperature settings. One retailer said its refrigeration units ran at 50 °C for about six hours. Le Monde reported defrost cycles lengthened from one hour to as many as eight, which delayed alarms. At one store near Bordeaux, a freezer setpoint was changed from −22 °C to 150 °C, with several settings altered per second, which points to automation.
  • Consequences. Food losses are estimated at tens of millions of euros. The Paris prosecutor's office said the attack also caused fires.
  • A second wave. On the night of 12 to 13 October the campaign reached controllers from a second manufacturer. CERT-FR updated its alert to cover them.
  • Access. How the attackers got in has not been publicly confirmed. Researchers counted thousands of refrigeration controller interfaces reachable from the internet, many of them on end of life systems.
  • Attribution. On 9 November France publicly attributed the operation to a group it links to Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Tehran rejected the accusation.

What CERT-FR recommends

  • Take the affected controllers off the internet.
  • Restore remote access only through a secure VPN or a protected network.
  • Use IP filtering only as a temporary emergency measure.
  • For compromised end of life devices, do not rely on a factory reset alone.
  • Review defrost schedules.

Removing exposure comes first. Monitoring does not replace it.

Our analysis

Calling these commands authorised is accurate about the supervisor and misleading about everything below it. The web login on a supervisory controller is the only place in this chain where a credential exists. The field protocols those supervisors speak downward, Modbus among them, carry no identity, no session and no integrity check: a write to a holding register is a function code and a value, accepted from whoever sends it. So there was never an authorisation decision to subvert at the point where the damage happened. Once an attacker is past the web layer they are not impersonating the process, they are the process.

The multi-vendor spread is the detail we would not pass over. CERT-FR named Copeland and Dixell, then updated the alert to add two Danfoss families. One campaign reaching controllers from different manufacturers across two nights is not a product defect being exploited; a bug in one vendor's firmware does not travel to another's. What the affected estates had in common was the exposure pattern, not the supplier. That matters for anyone reading vendor advisories to decide whether they are affected, because it means the advisory is the wrong document to read first. The right question is what of yours answers on the public internet.

Defrost was not an incidental choice of function. It is the one state in which the control logic itself expects temperature to rise, which is why defrost routinely inhibits or delays the high-temperature alarm. An attacker extending a cycle from one hour to eight, as reported, does not have to defeat the alarm strategy; the plant's own exception handling suppresses it for them. Any monitoring built on alarm state inherits that blindness by construction.

Then the setpoint itself. A freezer target moved from −22 °C to 150 °C tells you the controller applied no plausibility bound to a value it could never reach. In almost all of this equipment the engineering value is a scaled integer written straight to a register, with limits that exist in the technician's head rather than in the device, because the register was designed for someone who knows what the number means. A range check at the controller is the cheapest control anywhere in this incident, it needs no network, no licence and no project, and it is nearly always absent.

The signal that was available and unused was rate. Several settings altered per second at one store, and the same change pushed to many cabinets within seconds, are properties of the traffic rather than of any single message. Every individual write was valid and would pass any rule that inspects content, which is the reason signature-based thinking has nothing to offer here: there is no malicious payload to match, only a sequence that no technician would produce. Rate and fan-out can be observed without understanding the protocol semantics at all, which makes their absence from these estates a gap in instrumentation rather than in knowledge.

One line in the CERT-FR alert deserves more attention than it got: for compromised end of life devices, do not rely on a factory reset alone. A reset restores configuration. It does not reflash firmware, and on equipment of this age the boundary between the two is not where an administrator assumes it is. If a device was reachable, unsupported and is now suspect, the honest position is that its integrity cannot be established from the device itself.

Authorised is not always appropriate.

Questions

What happened in the French supermarket refrigeration attack?

On the night of 11 to 12 October 2026 attackers reached the refrigeration controllers of about 700 supermarkets in France and used defrost cycles and temperature setpoints to warm fridges and freezers overnight until the food was lost. A second wave the next night reached controllers from a second manufacturer.

How did the attackers get in?

It has not been publicly confirmed. Researchers counted thousands of refrigeration controller interfaces reachable from the internet, many of them on end of life systems. CERT-FR's first recommendation is to take the affected controllers off the internet.

How can an attack like this be detected?

Each command was authorised on its own, so the useful question is whether it makes sense for the process at that moment: a setpoint far outside the normal range, a defrost far longer than usual, the same change pushed to many cabinets within seconds. Removing exposure comes first; contextual monitoring adds a check on what the network is actually being told to do.
