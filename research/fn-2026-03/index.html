FIELD NOTES · FN-2026-03 · 18 November 2026

The update was the incident: telling a fault from an attack in a week that looked like one

In the middle of November 2026 an American air traffic shutdown, an outage on the National Security Agency's classified network and a run of unrelated service failures were assembled in public into a single story about an attack on American infrastructure. The two largest have published explanations, and both point at planned maintenance. One of them was a programme to replace ageing telecommunications equipment, which is the thing every security framework tells an operator to do. Its root cause has still not been established.

Key points

  • The FAA halted departures across the United States after a communications failure that affected communications and radar feeds at 17 of the country's 22 en route air traffic control centres. The White House said there was no indication of a nefarious act.
  • Both of the week's largest outages point at planned maintenance. The FAA failure came during a programme to replace ageing telecommunications equipment, and the NSA described a temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance.
  • The FAA has not established a definitive root cause. Under the Polish national cyber security act an operator has 24 hours to say whether an incident was caused by unlawful or bad faith action, if such an assessment is possible, and 72 hours to describe its causes.
  • A maintenance window is a detection blind spot, because during it every anomaly already has an explanation. Monitoring should be raised during planned work rather than reduced, and the people making the change should not be the only ones watching it.

What happened

  • The ground stop. The United States Federal Aviation Administration halted departures nationwide after a communications failure that affected communications and radar feeds at 17 of the country's 22 en route air traffic control centres. The White House said there was no indication of a "nefarious act".
  • The work being done. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said upgrade work on an old telecommunications system could be a cause, and that the contractor L3Harris was performing routine maintenance to switch telecommunications lines from old copper wire to fibre in at least one remote FAA location.
  • The equipment. The FAA paused installation of Juniper routers while it investigates whether the failure came from defective equipment or from an installation or configuration error. A spokesperson for HPE, the manufacturer's parent company, said that "based on what we know and have been told by others, our equipment was working as configured", and that the FAA is assessing the configuration and performance of all infrastructure involved.
  • The scale of the migration. L3Harris plans to install 3,648 Juniper routers. By CNN's account of a senators' letter, 400 have been installed and 69 are operational.
  • No root cause. The FAA has not established a definitive root cause of the outage.
  • The second outage. NSA Net, the agency's internal network for classified communications, went offline on Tuesday 17 November, prompting staggered arrival times for some personnel at Fort Meade. An NSA spokesperson said the agency had experienced a "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance", and that while some internal systems were affected, mission critical capabilities remained fully operational. Nextgov reported, citing four sources, that the outage may be linked to a series of software upgrades.
  • The attack nobody led with. In the same week the content platform Optimizely was compromised and used to serve malicious code to its customers' sites, among them CNN, Fox News, the BBC and Salesforce. This was a genuine supply chain attack, and it was not the story that travelled.

One reported figure we could not confirm

Several outlets reported that the FAA's legacy equipment became unstable when its telecommunications network was flooded at a rate of around a thousand connection attempts every four to five seconds. We could not confirm that figure in a source we were able to read in full, so we are not resting any part of the analysis on it. It is worth stating anyway, because if it is accurate it describes a traffic pattern that is indistinguishable at the packet level from a denial of service attempt, produced by nothing more hostile than a router doing its job in an unexpected state.

What was settled before it was known

Two conclusions reached the public within hours. The first was that American infrastructure was under coordinated attack, assembled from unrelated incidents by sites that exist to produce that kind of assembly. The second was the official one, that there was no cyberattack. The FBI was brought in to confirm the systems had not been hacked.

The second conclusion is far better founded than the first, and it is still not the same thing as knowing. The definitive root cause remains unestablished, and an investigation that cannot yet say what caused the failure is not in a position to have exhausted what did not. Both narratives arrived ahead of the evidence, in opposite directions, and only one of them is being treated as the careless one.

Our analysis

At the level where this is actually decided, a flood and a fault produce the same artefacts. A device that re-registers in a loop, a link that flaps, a service that retries without backoff: on the wire these are a rising rate of connection attempts from a known address, which is also what a crude denial of service looks like from the inside. Nothing in the packet distinguishes them. What distinguishes them is context the network cannot supply, and the first piece of that context is always the same question. What changed? In most industrial sites the honest answer takes hours to assemble, because change records live in a contractor's email, a maintenance ticket and somebody's memory of a phone call. That is why a change record is a detection control and not administration. An operator who can answer "what changed" in two minutes has halved the triage problem before touching a packet capture.

"Our equipment was working as configured" is the most important sentence anyone said during the episode, and it is both true and useless, which is the usual condition of that sentence. It places the failure in the gap between what a device was told to do and what somebody assumed it would do. Almost every serious industrial outage lives in that gap rather than in a defect. The practical consequence is that a vendor statement of this shape closes nothing: it rules out a manufacturing fault and leaves the entire configuration and integration surface open, which is where the answer usually is. We have written variants of that sentence into reports ourselves, and it is always the beginning of the work.

The migration figures are the part an industrial reader should sit with. Three thousand six hundred and forty eight routers planned, four hundred installed, sixty nine operational. That is not a project with a cutover date; it is a network that will spend years in a hybrid state, carrying old copper and new fibre, old and new routing equipment, two configuration regimes and two sets of assumptions about what the other end will do. Hybrid states are where interface assumptions break, because each half was tested against a specification rather than against the half it actually has to talk to. Any engineer who has run a plant through a staged DCS migration recognises the shape immediately. The dangerous period in the life of a control system is not steady state operation and it is not the cutover weekend. It is the long middle, when both architectures are live and the documentation describes neither.

"No evidence of a cyberattack" deserves to be read as the statement about evidence that it is, rather than as a statement about the world. Its weight is exactly the weight of the telemetry behind it. In an enterprise network with full flow records and host logging it can be close to dispositive. In an industrial network it very often means that nothing was collected which could have shown an intrusion, and the honest rendering is that we would not have seen it. The distinction between no evidence found and no evidence possible is the one an operator has to be able to make about their own site, and it is not a comfortable exercise. It also has a legal edge now. Incident reporting duties under NIS2 and its national transpositions assume the operator can characterise what happened inside a short window, and an operator without the telemetry to tell a fault from an intrusion is not in a position to make that call honestly in either direction.

There is a reading of this week that cuts against how the update obligation is usually taught. Article 8 of the amended Polish national cyber security act requires regular software updates in accordance with the manufacturer's recommendations, taking into account an analysis of the update's impact on the security of the service and the criticality of individual updates. Read quickly, that sounds like a lawyer's hedge inserted so that operators have something to hide behind. The FAA programme is the demonstration that it is not. Replacing ageing telecommunications equipment is unambiguously the right thing to do, it is what every framework demands, and carrying it out took down seventeen of twenty two en route centres. The clause does not exist to excuse deferral. It exists because the impact analysis and the rollback plan are the parts that make the update survivable, and because an organisation that cannot say in advance what an update can break has not finished preparing it. The FAA disabled the two suspect routers and service returned. That is a rollback, and it worked. The question worth asking about your own site is not whether you patch quickly. It is whether, at three in the morning, anybody can state what backing this change out involves and how long it takes.

The last point follows from process rather than technology, and it is the one we would raise in a review. A maintenance window is a detection blind spot, because during it every anomaly has a ready explanation. Alarms are expected, links drop by design, services restart, and the standing instruction to the people watching is not to chase what the work is already known to be causing. An adversary who understands a target's change calendar does not need an exploit that beats the defences on an ordinary Tuesday; they need to be inside the window, where their traffic is pre-attributed to the contractor. Most industrial sites handle this exactly backwards, reducing monitoring during planned work to cut noise. It should be raised, and the people performing the change should not be the only people watching it. Separating those two roles costs a shift and closes a gap that no product closes.

Questions

What caused the November 2026 FAA ground stop?

The FAA has not established a definitive root cause. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said upgrade work on an old telecommunications system could be a cause, and that the contractor L3Harris was switching telecommunications lines from old copper wire to fibre in at least one remote location. The FAA paused installation of Juniper routers while it investigates whether the failure came from defective equipment or from an installation or configuration error.

Was it a cyberattack?

Officials said there was no indication of one and the FBI was brought in to confirm the systems had not been hacked. That is better founded than the claims of a coordinated attack that circulated at the same time, but it is not the same as knowing: an investigation that cannot yet say what caused a failure is not in a position to have exhausted what did not.

What does an American air traffic failure have to do with industrial control systems?

The shape of the problem is identical. At the packet level a device re-registering in a loop and a crude denial of service attempt look the same, and what separates them is context the network cannot supply. The first question in triage is what changed, which is why a change record functions as a detection control rather than as administration.

How quickly does Polish law require an incident to be reported?

The amended national cyber security act requires an early warning to the sector CSIRT without delay and no later than 24 hours from detection, a full notification within 72 hours, a periodic report on request, and a final report within a month of the notification. The early warning must state whether the incident was caused by unlawful or bad faith action, if such an assessment is possible, and the 72 hour notification must describe its causes.

What should an operator do differently during planned maintenance?

Raise monitoring rather than reduce it, and separate the people performing the change from the people watching it. During a maintenance window every anomaly has a ready explanation, which is precisely why an adversary who knows the change calendar does not need an exploit that beats the defences on an ordinary day.
